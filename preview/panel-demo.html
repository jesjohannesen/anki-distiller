The box that built the world

By A. Writer · Example Review

When Malcom McLean loaded fifty-eight aluminium boxes onto a converted tanker in 1956, the cost of loading loose cargo onto a ship ran to about $5.86 a ton. Using containers, the same work cost sixteen cents. That single comparison, repeated in every history of the industry since, is usually offered as an argument about ships. It is really an argument about ports.

Break-bulk shipping was labour, not steel. Sacks, barrels and crates arrived at the quay in whatever shape their manufacturer had chosen, and gangs of longshoremen stowed them by hand into the irregular spaces of a hold. A ship might spend longer alongside than at sea. Freight rates therefore tracked the price of dockside labour far more closely than they tracked fuel or hull size.

Standardisation was the hard part. A container only pays for itself if the same box fits the ship, the crane, the railcar and the chassis, which means agreement across industries that had no history of talking to each other, and which had already sunk capital into equipment sized for something else. The economics that made containers obviously right for shippers made them obviously wrong for the ports and unions being asked to pay for the change.

The lesson generalises past shipping. A transport innovation lowers landed cost in proportion to the share of cost it removes at the transfer points between modes, not in proportion to how fast or how large the vehicle itself becomes. Faster ships were available all along. Cheaper transfers were not.
